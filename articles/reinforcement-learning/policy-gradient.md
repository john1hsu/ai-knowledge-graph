# Policy gradient methods

Policy gradient methods are a family of reinforcement learning (RL) algorithms that skip the step of deriving behaviour from a learned value function. Instead, the policy itself gets its own function approximator, with parameters θ, and those parameters are adjusted along the gradient of expected reward.[^s1] Because the policy is typically stochastic, a small change in θ produces only a small change in behaviour. A greedy policy read off an estimated value function can instead jump abruptly between actions when estimates shift slightly.[^s1]

## Origin

The earliest widely cited member of the family is REINFORCE. Ronald J. Williams introduced it in a 1992 *Machine Learning* paper as a class of algorithms for networks of stochastic units. Their weight updates follow the gradient of expected reinforcement without explicitly computing or storing that gradient.[^s2] Williams also showed that REINFORCE can be combined with backpropagation. He noted, however, that the gradient guarantee held fully only for immediate reinforcement and for limited forms of delayed reinforcement.[^s2] In 1999, Sutton, McAllester, Singh and Mansour gave the general **policy gradient theorem** at NIPS. They proved that a version of policy iteration with arbitrary differentiable function approximation converges to a locally optimal policy.[^s1]

## How it works

The objective is the expected return J(π_θ) of trajectories generated by the policy. Applying the log-derivative trick, ∇_θ P = P ∇_θ log P, rewrites the gradient of this expectation as an expectation that can be sampled. The environment's dynamics and initial-state distribution drop out because they do not depend on θ.[^s3] The basic estimator is:

∇_θ J ≈ (1/N) Σ_trajectories Σ_t ∇_θ log π_θ(a_t | s_t) · Ψ_t

Here Ψ_t is a weight on each action. In the simplest form it is the whole episode's return. Intuitively, the update raises the probability of actions that led to high return and lowers it for actions that led to low return.[^s4] The policy gradient theorem shows that the true gradient does not involve how policy changes alter the state distribution. That is why it can be estimated from experience alone.[^s1]

Practical variants lower the variance of this estimate. *Reward-to-go* weights each action only by rewards that come after it, since earlier rewards add noise but no signal. Subtracting a state-dependent **baseline** b(s_t) leaves the gradient unbiased, because the expected grad-log-probability is zero.[^s3] The usual baseline is a learned value function V(s). Weighting by an *advantage* estimate A(s,a) ≈ Q(s,a) − V(s) then measures how much better an action was than the policy's average.[^s3][^s4] Sutton et al. showed that a "compatible" learned approximator can stand in for the true action values without biasing the gradient. This result underpins actor-critic methods, in which a critic estimates values for the actor (the policy).[^s1]

## Limitations

The estimators have high variance and need many samples. Training can also be unstable, because each policy update changes the distribution of data the policy then learns from.[^s5] Generalized Advantage Estimation (GAE) tackles the variance with an exponentially weighted advantage estimator, similar to TD(λ), and accepts some bias in return.[^s5] Vanilla policy gradient is on-policy, so data must come from the current policy. It explores only by sampling from its own stochastic policy, which becomes more deterministic over training. As a result it can get stuck in local optima.[^s4] OpenAI's tutorial also cautions that the surrogate "loss" coded for these methods is not a performance measure. Practitioners should track average return instead.[^s3]

## In practice

Vanilla policy gradient supports both discrete and continuous action spaces.[^s4] Its descendants dominate continuous control. GAE combined with trust-region updates learned simulated bipedal and quadrupedal running gaits from raw kinematic inputs in 2015.[^s5] Proximal Policy Optimization (Schulman et al., 2017) replaced the one-gradient-step-per-sample update with several epochs of minibatch updates on a clipped surrogate objective. The authors reported better sample complexity than other online policy gradient methods on robotics and Atari benchmarks.[^s6]

## Connections

Policy gradients are one of the two main approaches to solving a **reinforcement learning** problem framed as a **Markov decision process**. The other derives a policy from learned values, as **Q-learning** does. The 1999 paper motivated the policy-gradient route partly by citing convergence failures of value-based methods under function approximation.[^s1] The method **is based on** Williams's **REINFORCE** and uses **backpropagation** to compute grad-log-probabilities through neural policies.[^s2] Adding a learned critic produces **actor-critic** methods.[^s1] Building on that line, the family **enables** **Proximal Policy Optimization**.[^s6] Through PPO, policy gradients are also **used in** **reinforcement learning from human feedback**, whose node in this graph records PPO as a dependency for its policy-optimization stage.
