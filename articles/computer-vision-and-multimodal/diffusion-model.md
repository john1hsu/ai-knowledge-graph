# Diffusion model

A **diffusion model** is a generative model that learns a data distribution by learning to undo noise. A fixed *forward* process slowly destroys the structure of a training example by adding Gaussian noise over many steps until nothing but noise remains; a neural network then learns the *reverse* process, removing a little noise at a time. To generate, the model starts from random noise and applies the learned denoising steps until a new sample emerges.[^s1][^s2] By 2022 they were achieving state-of-the-art image synthesis and had been extended to text-conditioned generation.[^s4]

## Origin

The idea was introduced by Jascha Sohl-Dickstein, Eric Weiss, Niru Maheswaranathan and Surya Ganguli at ICML 2015. Borrowing from nonequilibrium statistical physics, they proposed systematically eroding structure in data with a diffusion process and learning a reverse process that restores it, yielding a model that is both flexible and tractable for sampling and likelihood evaluation.[^s1] The approach drew wide attention after Jonathan Ho, Ajay Jain and Pieter Abbeel (UC Berkeley) published *Denoising Diffusion Probabilistic Models* (DDPM) at NeurIPS 2020, showing image quality competitive with the best generative adversarial networks of the time.[^s2]

## How it works

In DDPM the forward process is a Markov chain with a variance schedule β₁…β_T: each step samples x_t from a Gaussian with mean √(1−β_t)·x_{t−1} and variance β_t. The paper used T = 1000 steps with β rising linearly from 10⁻⁴ to 0.02. Writing α_t = 1−β_t and ᾱ_t for the running product of the α values, any noisy version can be drawn in one shot: x_t = √ᾱ_t·x₀ + √(1−ᾱ_t)·ε, with ε standard Gaussian noise.[^s2]

Training is then simple. Pick a clean example x₀, a random step t and random noise ε; form x_t; and train a network ε_θ(x_t, t) to predict ε with a mean-squared-error loss. Ho et al. found this simplified objective gave better samples than optimizing the full variational bound, and showed that predicting noise is equivalent to denoising score matching across many noise levels, so that sampling resembles annealed Langevin dynamics.[^s2] Generation runs the chain backwards: starting from pure noise x_T, the network's noise estimate is used to compute x_{T−1}, then x_{T−2}, and so on down to x₀.[^s2]

Song et al. (ICLR 2021) generalized this to continuous time. A stochastic differential equation (SDE) maps data to a simple prior, and a reverse-time SDE that depends only on the *score* (the gradient of the log-density) maps noise back to data. The framework unifies earlier score-based and diffusion models, adds predictor–corrector samplers, and yields an equivalent ordinary differential equation that allows exact likelihoods and faster sampling.[^s3]

## Results and limitations

DDPM reported an unconditional CIFAR-10 FID of 3.17 and Inception Score of 9.46 in 2020, and LSUN 256×256 samples of quality similar to ProgressiveGAN.[^s2] The SDE paper pushed CIFAR-10 to an FID of 2.20 and Inception Score of 9.89, and produced the first high-fidelity 1024×1024 images from a score-based model.[^s3]

The main costs are compute and speed. DDPM sampling needs one network evaluation per step—1,000 in the original setup—so a batch of 256 CIFAR-10 images took about 17 seconds on a TPU v3-8, far slower than a single GAN forward pass.[^s2] Its authors also noted that log-likelihoods were not competitive with other likelihood-based models.[^s2] Rombach et al. observed that training pixel-space diffusion models can consume hundreds of GPU days and that inference is expensive.[^s4]

## In practice

The most influential efficiency fix is the **latent diffusion model** (CVPR 2022): run diffusion in the compressed latent space of a pretrained autoencoder instead of on pixels, and add cross-attention layers so that text, bounding boxes or other inputs can condition generation. This cut compute substantially while matching or setting state-of-the-art results on inpainting, class-conditional synthesis, text-to-image and super-resolution.[^s4] In open-source tooling, Hugging Face's Diffusers library separates the denoising network from a *scheduler*, the algorithm that decides how much noise to remove at each step; samplers such as DDIM and DPM++ trade accuracy against speed, and tuned timestep schedules can produce good images in around 10 steps rather than hundreds (as documented in 2026).[^s5]

## Connections

Diffusion models are a family of **generative models** within **deep learning**, introduced in Sohl-Dickstein et al.'s 2015 paper.[^s1] They are the main **alternative to generative adversarial networks**, matching GAN sample quality in 2020 at the price of many sequential denoising steps instead of a single forward pass.[^s2] Their training objective is **based on score matching**, the link formalized by the SDE view.[^s2][^s3] Like **variational autoencoders**, they are trained with a variational bound, and **latent diffusion** combines the two by diffusing in an autoencoder's latent space, the design behind much **text-to-image generation**.[^s2][^s4] Practical implementations are **available in Hugging Face Diffusers**.[^s5]
