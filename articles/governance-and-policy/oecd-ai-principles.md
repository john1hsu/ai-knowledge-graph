# OECD AI Principles

The **OECD AI Principles** are the content of the OECD Council's *Recommendation on Artificial Intelligence* (legal instrument OECD/LEGAL/0449), which the OECD describes as the first intergovernmental standard on AI.[^s1][^s3] The Council adopted it at ministerial level on 22 May 2019, on a proposal from what is now the Digital Policy Committee, and amended it on 8 November 2023 (a new definition of "AI system") and on 3 May 2024 (a broader revision prompted by general-purpose and generative AI).[^s1][^s2] An OECD Recommendation is not a treaty: it is a political commitment by the governments that adhere to it, and press coverage at launch described it as strong but non-binding.[^s5]

## Origin

The text was drafted with input from an OECD expert group of more than 50 members, drawn from about 20 governments plus industry, academia and science, which met four times between September 2018 and February 2019.[^s5] At launch, 42 countries signed on: the then 36 OECD members plus Argentina, Brazil, Colombia, Costa Rica, Peru and Romania.[^s5] In June 2019, G20 leaders at the Osaka Summit welcomed a set of G20 AI Principles drawn from the Recommendation, which extended its reach to large non-OECD economies.[^s1][^s3] As of the May 2024 revision, the OECD counted 47 adherents, including the European Union.[^s2]

## Structure

Section 1 sets out five values-based principles for the responsible stewardship of trustworthy AI, addressed to all "AI actors" (the organisations and people who take an active part in an AI system's lifecycle):[^s1]

1. Inclusive growth, sustainable development and well-being.
2. Respect for the rule of law, human rights and democratic values, including fairness and privacy.
3. Transparency and explainability, so that people affected can understand and challenge outputs.
4. Robustness, security and safety, including the ability to override, repair or decommission a system.
5. Accountability, supported by traceability and systematic risk management.[^s1]

Section 2 gives five recommendations to governments: invest in AI research and development; foster an inclusive, AI-enabling ecosystem of data and infrastructure; shape an enabling, interoperable governance and policy environment; build human capacity and prepare for labour-market transformation; and co-operate internationally on trustworthy AI, including standards and comparable indicators.[^s1] The Digital Policy Committee, working through its Working Party on AI Governance, is charged with monitoring implementation and reporting back to the Council.[^s1]

## The AI-system definition

The Recommendation's definition is arguably its most reused part. In its 2023 form, an AI system is a machine-based system that, for explicit or implicit objectives, infers from its inputs how to generate outputs such as predictions, content, recommendations or decisions that can influence physical or virtual environments; systems vary in autonomy and in adaptiveness after deployment.[^s1] Stuart Russell, Karine Perset and Marko Grobelnik explained that the revision replaced "human-defined objectives" with explicit or implicit ones (covering systems like large language models), added "content" to make generative AI clearly in scope, and changed "real" to "physical" environments because virtual ones are also real.[^s4] The OECD reports that the EU, the Council of Europe, the United States and the United Nations use its definition and lifecycle in laws, regulations or guidance.[^s3]

## 2024 revision

The 2024 update tightened the safety principle around mechanisms to override, repair or safely decommission systems posing undue risk; added attention to mis- and disinformation and information integrity; introduced responsible business conduct across the lifecycle; clarified what transparency and responsible disclosure mean; named environmental sustainability explicitly; and called for interoperable governance as national AI policies proliferate.[^s2]

## Limitations

Because adherence is voluntary and the principles are high-level, they say what trustworthy AI should achieve rather than how to test or certify it; operational detail is left to national law, standards and risk frameworks.[^s1][^s5] Several G20 members, including China and India, have committed to the G20 AI Principles but are not adherents to the OECD instrument itself.[^s3] The OECD supports implementation through its OECD.AI Policy Observatory, which tracks more than 900 national AI policies and initiatives (figure as shown on the site in November 2026).[^s3]

## Connections

The Principles are a foundational text of **AI governance** (`broader`), and were produced by the **OECD** (`createdBy`).[^s1] Their AI-system definition and lifecycle feed directly into the **EU AI Act** and US guidance such as the **NIST AI Risk Management Framework**, which share the same trustworthiness vocabulary.[^s3][^s4] The accountability principle calls for systematic **AI risk management**, and the human-rights principle's emphasis on fairness links to work on **algorithmic bias and fairness**.[^s1][^s2]
