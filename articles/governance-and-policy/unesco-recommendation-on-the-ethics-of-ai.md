# UNESCO Recommendation on the Ethics of Artificial Intelligence

The **UNESCO Recommendation on the Ethics of Artificial Intelligence** is a global standard-setting instrument adopted by the 41st session of UNESCO's General Conference in Paris on 23 November 2021.[^s1] UN News reported it as the first global agreement on AI ethics, adopted by all of UNESCO's then 193 Member States.[^s2] Like other UNESCO Recommendations it is not a treaty: the General Conference recommends that Member States apply its provisions on a voluntary basis, through legislation or other measures suited to their own constitutional practice.[^s1]

## Origin and drafting

The 40th General Conference mandated the instrument in November 2019.[^s1] In March 2020 UNESCO convened an Ad Hoc Expert Group of 24 independent experts, chosen for balance across UNESCO's regions, who drafted a first text online over six weeks because of the COVID-19 pandemic.[^s3] Consultation from June to August 2020 combined a public online survey (more than 600 completed responses and over 50,000 comments), 11 regional and sub-regional virtual consultations, and citizen deliberation workshops with roughly 500 participants.[^s3] The draft went to Member States in September 2020, drew written comments from 48 of them, and was then negotiated by an intergovernmental special committee in April and June 2021 before going to the General Conference.[^s3]

## What it contains

The Recommendation deliberately declines to fix a single definition of AI, arguing that any definition would need to change as the technology does. Instead it treats AI systems as information-processing technologies that integrate models and algorithms, can behave in ways resembling intelligence (reasoning, learning, perception, prediction, planning or control) and operate with varying autonomy; it addresses all "AI actors" across the system life cycle.[^s1]

Its normative core has three layers:[^s1]

- **Four values:** human rights, fundamental freedoms and human dignity; environment and ecosystem flourishing; diversity and inclusiveness; and peaceful, just and interconnected societies.[^s1]
- **Ten principles:** proportionality and do no harm; safety and security; fairness and non-discrimination; sustainability; privacy and data protection; human oversight and determination; transparency and explainability; responsibility and accountability; awareness and literacy; and multi-stakeholder, adaptive governance.[^s1]
- **Eleven policy action areas:** ethical impact assessment; ethical governance and stewardship; data policy; development and international cooperation; environment and ecosystems; gender; culture; education and research; communication and information; economy and labour; and health and social well-being.[^s1]

Some provisions are notably concrete. Under proportionality, it states that AI systems should not be used for social scoring or mass surveillance.[^s1][^s2] It holds that an AI system "can never replace ultimate human responsibility and accountability", that life-and-death decisions should as a rule not be ceded to AI, and that AI systems should not be given legal personality.[^s1] The environment chapter asks AI actors to favour data-, energy- and resource-efficient methods and to apply the precautionary principle.[^s1] Gender equality likewise receives its own policy area rather than being folded into non-discrimination.[^s1]

## Implementation tools

Paragraph 49 commits UNESCO to developing a readiness assessment methodology and an ethical impact assessment methodology to help Member States act on the text.[^s1] The Readiness Assessment Methodology (RAM), published in 2023, is a macro-level diagnostic covering five dimensions (legal/regulatory, social/cultural, economic, scientific/educational and technological/infrastructural); UNESCO stresses that its aim is to find gaps and shape national roadmaps, not to rank countries.[^s4] In its November 2025 submission to the UN Commission on Science and Technology for Development, UNESCO said the RAM was being implemented in over 70 countries and described an EU-funded project piloting RAM and ethical impact assessment in 18 countries, plus an "AI Ethics Experts Without Borders" network of more than 100 experts.[^s5] The Recommendation itself leaves monitoring largely to Member States, suggesting options such as ethics commissions, observatories and regulatory sandboxes rather than a fixed reporting cycle.[^s1]

## Limitations and critiques

Because it is voluntary and principle-level, the Recommendation's effect depends on national uptake. The ITU's 2025 AI governance report describes implementation as still at an early stage, with UNESCO's diagnostic work concentrated in Africa, the Caribbean and Latin America.[^s6] Its breadth, spanning culture, labour, health and the environment, makes it a wide ethical frame but gives less operational guidance than risk-management standards or binding regulation.[^s1][^s6] Country counts also vary by source and date: UN News cited 193 Member States at adoption, while the ITU report refers to 194.[^s2][^s6]

## Connections

The Recommendation is a key text of **AI ethics** and **AI governance** (`broader`), produced by **UNESCO** (`createdBy`), and it contains the **ethical impact assessment** mechanism as one of its policy areas (`hasPart`).[^s1] It sits alongside the **OECD AI Principles** as one of the main non-binding intergovernmental frameworks, whereas the **EU AI Act** and the **Council of Europe Framework Convention on AI** are binding instruments that turn similar values into legal obligations.[^s1][^s6] Its fairness and non-discrimination principle connects to work on **algorithmic bias and fairness**, and its proportionality and impact-assessment provisions overlap with **AI risk management** practice.[^s1][^s4]
