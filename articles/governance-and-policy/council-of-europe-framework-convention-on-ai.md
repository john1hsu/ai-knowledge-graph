# Council of Europe Framework Convention on Artificial Intelligence

The **Council of Europe Framework Convention on Artificial Intelligence and Human Rights, Democracy and the Rule of Law** (Council of Europe Treaty Series No. 225) is an international treaty whose stated purpose is to ensure that activities across the lifecycle of AI systems are fully consistent with human rights, democracy and the rule of law.[^s1] The Council of Europe's Committee of Ministers adopted it in Strasbourg on 17 May 2024, and the Council presents it as the first legally binding international treaty in the field.[^s2] Unlike the non-binding OECD AI Principles or a regional regulation such as the EU AI Act, it is a treaty that states (and the European Union) join by signature and ratification, and it is open to countries outside Europe.[^s3][^s5]

## Origin and negotiation

The text was drafted over roughly two years by the Committee on Artificial Intelligence (CAI), an intergovernmental body set up in 2022 as successor to the earlier ad hoc committee CAHAI, which had begun work in 2019.[^s4][^s2] Negotiators included the 46 Council of Europe member states, the European Union and 11 non-member states, among them the United States, Canada, Japan, Israel, Australia and several Latin American countries, with industry, civil society and academia attending as observers.[^s2] The treaty opened for signature at a conference of justice ministers in Vilnius on 5 September 2024; the first signatories were Andorra, Georgia, Iceland, Norway, Moldova, San Marino, the United Kingdom, Israel, the United States and the European Union.[^s3][^s4]

## What it requires

The convention defines an AI system in terms close to the OECD's revised definition: a machine-based system that infers from inputs how to produce outputs such as predictions, content, recommendations or decisions that can influence physical or virtual environments.[^s1] Its core obligations are framed as principles that parties must implement in domestic law, graduated by the severity and likelihood of harm:[^s1][^s2]

- protecting human dignity and individual autonomy, equality and non-discrimination, and privacy and personal data (Articles 7, 10, 11);[^s1]
- transparency and oversight tailored to context and risk, including identification of AI-generated content (Article 8);[^s1]
- accountability for adverse impacts, reliability of systems, and supervised testing environments for safe innovation (Articles 9, 12, 13);[^s1]
- safeguarding democratic processes, separation of powers, judicial independence and access to justice (Article 5).[^s2]

Parties must provide accessible remedies, including documentation that lets affected people contest AI-influenced decisions, and must tell people when they are interacting with an AI system rather than a human (Articles 14–15).[^s1] Article 16 requires an iterative risk and impact management framework, with testing before first use and after significant changes, and asks parties to assess whether moratoria or bans are needed for uses incompatible with human rights.[^s1] To support that work, the CAI has been developing HUDERIA, a non-binding human rights, democracy and rule-of-law impact assessment methodology.[^s4]

## Scope, exclusions and critiques

The treaty applies fully to public authorities and private actors acting on their behalf; for other private-sector activity, each party declares whether it will apply the principles directly or meet them through other measures.[^s1][^s2] National security activities need not be covered (though they must respect international law), national defence is outside scope, and research and development on systems not yet available for use is excluded unless testing could interfere with rights, democracy or the rule of law.[^s1] Rotenberg's note flags the features that make its obligations less direct than a regulation's: the private-sector option, the security and defence carve-outs, and qualified wording such as "seek to ensure" or "as appropriate".[^s5]

## Implementation

Compliance is followed up by a Conference of the Parties, convened by the Council's Secretary General, which supports implementation, exchanges information, helps settle disputes and considers amendments; each party must also designate independent oversight mechanisms.[^s1][^s2] The convention enters into force on the first day of the month following three months after five signatories, at least three of them Council of Europe member states, have ratified it.[^s1][^s3] Because ratification is ongoing, its current status should be checked against the Council of Europe Treaty Office.

## Connections

The convention sits under **AI governance** as its first binding multilateral instrument.[^s5] It was **created by** the **Council of Europe** through the CAI.[^s2] It is **influenced by** the **OECD AI Principles**, whose AI-system definition it closely tracks, and Rotenberg's note places it in a lineage running from the OECD principles and UNESCO's ethics recommendation to the **EU AI Act**, which he describes as mutually influencing the convention.[^s5] Its non-discrimination article links it to **algorithmic bias and fairness**, and its Article 16 risk framework connects it to **AI risk management** practices.[^s1]
