# Multi-agent systems

A **multi-agent system (MAS)** is a system composed of several agents that interact with one another, where each agent is a computational system able to act independently on behalf of a user or owner, deciding for itself what to do to meet its objectives. Because agents may represent parties with different goals, a MAS has to support cooperation, coordination and negotiation, and its interactions can look more like economic encounters than like function calls.[^s1] The idea long predates large language models: Michael Wooldridge's textbook *An Introduction to MultiAgent Systems* (2002) framed the field as a response to computing trends toward ubiquity, networked interconnection, delegation of ever more complex tasks, and more human-oriented programming abstractions.[^s1]

Since 2023 the term has been revived for **LLM-based multi-agent systems**, in which each agent is an LLM instance given a profile (role), its own context, and often tools. A 2024 IJCAI survey describes the shift from single LLM agents to multi-agent setups for complex problem solving and for simulating environments.[^s2]

## How it works

Guo et al. characterise LLM multi-agent systems along four design axes:[^s3]

- **Agents–environment interface:** agents act in a sandbox (a code interpreter or game), in the physical world (robotics), or in no external environment at all, as when they only talk to each other.[^s3]
- **Profiling:** agent roles are written by designers, generated by an LLM, or derived from datasets.[^s3]
- **Communication:** paradigms are cooperative, debate-style or competitive; structures are layered, decentralized (peer-to-peer), centralized around a coordinator, or a shared message pool that agents publish to and subscribe from.[^s3]
- **Capability acquisition:** agents learn from environment, peer or human feedback, and adapt through memory, self-evolution, or dynamic generation of new agents at run time.[^s3]

Two patterns illustrate the range. In **multiagent debate**, several instances of the same model each propose an answer, then over several rounds read and critique one another's reasoning until they converge; Du et al. reported at ICML 2024 that this "society of minds" procedure improved mathematical and strategic reasoning and reduced factual errors, using identical prompts across tasks and only black-box model access.[^s4] In the **orchestrator–worker** pattern, a lead agent plans, spawns subagents that work in parallel with separate context windows, and merges their results; Anthropic's research feature, described in June 2025, uses this design with an extra agent that attaches citations.[^s5]

## Evidence, costs and limitations

Reported gains are real but conditional. Anthropic stated that a lead Claude Opus 4 agent with Claude Sonnet 4 subagents scored 90.2% higher than a single Opus 4 agent on its internal research evaluation, but also that multi-agent runs used roughly 15 times the tokens of a chat interaction, and that token usage alone explained about 80% of performance variance on the BrowseComp benchmark (all figures as reported by Anthropic in 2025).[^s5] The same write-up found the approach poorly suited to tasks where all agents need shared context or have many interdependencies, including most coding work, and described early failures such as spawning dozens of subagents for simple queries and duplicating work after vague delegation.[^s5]

Systematic studies point the same way. Cemri et al. analysed seven popular multi-agent frameworks across more than 200 tasks and derived 14 failure modes grouped into specification issues, inter-agent misalignment, and task verification, with annotator agreement of κ = 0.88 (arXiv preprint, 2025).[^s6] The IJCAI survey lists open problems including hallucinations that propagate between agents, the cost and coordination overhead of scaling agent counts, the lack of benchmarks for group-level rather than individual behaviour, and the difficulty of jointly improving agents toward collective intelligence.[^s2][^s3]

## In practice

Practitioners use LLM multi-agent designs for parallelisable research and search, role-based software development, and social or economic simulation, the two broad application families the survey identifies as problem solving and world simulation.[^s3] Because of the cost and coordination risks above, a common engineering judgement is to reserve multi-agent designs for high-value, breadth-first tasks and to invest heavily in clear delegation prompts, evaluation and durable execution.[^s5]

## Connections

- Narrower than **AI agents** and **agentic systems**: a MAS is a collection of agents plus the protocol by which they interact.[^s1]
- **AutoGen** and **LangGraph** are frameworks that implement multi-agent conversation and orchestration graphs, and **the Agent2Agent protocol** standardises messaging between agents built on different stacks; together they realise the communication structures described above.[^s3]
- **Generative Agents (Park et al.)** is a landmark world-simulation MAS.[^s3]
- Multiagent debate is a cousin of **self-consistency** (sampling and aggregating many answers) and of debate-based **scalable oversight**, although Du et al. use debate to improve accuracy rather than to supervise stronger models.[^s4]
- Agents can amplify one another's **hallucinations**, a risk the survey singles out for multi-agent settings.[^s3]
- The classic theory traces to **Michael Wooldridge**'s work on agent cooperation and negotiation.[^s1]
