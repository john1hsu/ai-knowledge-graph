# BM25

**BM25** (often "Okapi BM25") is a ranking function for keyword search. Given a query, it gives each document a relevance score by adding up one weight per query term that the document contains; each weight combines how rare the term is across the collection with how often it appears in the document, adjusted for the document's length.[^s1] It is the best-known product of the **probabilistic relevance framework** developed by Stephen Robertson, Karen Spärck Jones and colleagues, and it remains the standard lexical baseline against which neural retrievers are compared.[^s1][^s3]

## Origin

The framework traces back to Maron and Kuhns's probabilistic indexing work of 1960, Harter's 2-Poisson model of term "eliteness" (1975) and the Robertson/Spärck Jones relevance weight of 1977.[^s1] BM25 itself was built at City University London for the **Okapi** retrieval system. In the team's TREC-3 paper, Robertson, Walker and co-authors described BM25 as a single family that unifies two earlier functions, BM11 and BM15, through a new tunable parameter *b*: setting *b* = 1 recovers BM11 and *b* = 0 recovers BM15.[^s2] Their official ad hoc run at TREC-3, using BM25 together with query expansion and passage retrieval, reached an average precision of 0.401, compared with 0.337 for their unexpanded BM25 run.[^s2] Robertson and Zaragoza's 2009 survey later presented BM25, and its multi-field extension BM25F, as the framework's main practical outcome.[^s1]

## How it works

For a query term with document frequency *n* in a collection of *N* documents, BM25 uses an inverse-document-frequency weight that Robertson and Zaragoza note closely approximates classical idf when no relevance judgments are available:[^s1]

  idf = log((N − n + 0.5) / (n + 0.5))

That weight is multiplied by a term-frequency component:[^s1][^s2]

  tf / (k1 · ((1 − b) + b · dl / avdl) + tf)

where *tf* is the term's count in the document, *dl* the document length and *avdl* the average length. The document's score is the sum over query terms.[^s1]

Two parameters do the work:

- **k1 (saturation).** Because *tf* appears in both numerator and denominator, each extra occurrence adds less than the last, and one term's contribution can never exceed a ceiling. Larger *k1* makes saturation slower.[^s1][^s4]
- **b (length normalization).** At *b* = 1, term counts are fully normalized by relative document length; at *b* = 0 length is ignored. Robertson and Zaragoza frame this as a compromise between two explanations of long documents: wordiness (which should be discounted) and broader scope (which should not).[^s1]

A worked intuition: a rare term such as "photosynthesis" has a high idf, so even one occurrence lifts a document a lot, while a fifth occurrence adds only a little more; a common word like "the" contributes almost nothing. The original Okapi formulation also included a query-term-frequency factor (parameter *k3*) and an optional global length correction (*k2*), which most modern implementations drop.[^s2]

## Limitations and open questions

BM25 is purely lexical: it can only reward exact term matches, so it misses synonyms and paraphrases, the gap that **semantic search** with dense embeddings targets.[^s3] The model itself gives no guidance on setting *k1* and *b*; they must be tuned empirically, and the best values depend on document and query type. Robertson and Zaragoza report that 0.5 < *b* < 0.8 and 1.2 < *k1* < 2 work reasonably well in many settings.[^s1] The framework treats relevance as binary, assumes terms are independent (which its authors call a clearly arguable step) and largely ignores word positions.[^s1]

Yet it is hard to beat out of domain. The BEIR benchmark (Thakur and colleagues, 2021) evaluated ten retrieval systems zero-shot on 18 datasets and found BM25 a robust baseline, while dense retrievers, though efficient, often underperformed it when moved to new domains.[^s3]

## In practice

BM25 is the default relevance function in the major open-source search engines. The Apache Lucene project switched its default similarity from classic TF-IDF to BM25 (LUCENE-6789), renaming the old default `ClassicSimilarity`, and Solr followed for version 6.0.[^s5] Elasticsearch, built on Lucene, uses BM25 by default with *k1* = 1.2 and *b* = 0.75, as documented by Elastic in 2018.[^s4] Because it needs no training data and runs on an ordinary inverted index, it is a common first-stage retriever: BEIR's comparison spans lexical, sparse, dense, late-interaction and re-ranking systems, with the more expensive re-ranking and late-interaction models scoring best at higher compute cost.[^s3] In LLM applications the same role appears as the keyword half of hybrid search or the first stage of retrieval-augmented generation.[^s3]

## Connections

BM25 sits within **information retrieval** and is **based on** the **probabilistic relevance framework**, whose idf-like Robertson/Spärck Jones weight it inherits.[^s1] It **extends** the older **TF-IDF** weighting by adding term-frequency saturation and document length normalization, and it replaced TF-IDF as Lucene's default.[^s1][^s5] It is the main **alternative to** dense **semantic search** over **embeddings**, and the two are often combined in **hybrid search** and followed by **reranking**.[^s3] Its zero-shot strength is documented by its results **on BEIR**, and it is **implemented in Apache Lucene** and the engines built on it, which makes it a common first stage in **retrieval-augmented generation**.[^s3][^s5][^s4]
