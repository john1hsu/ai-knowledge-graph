# Dartmouth workshop

The **Dartmouth workshop**, formally the Dartmouth Summer Research Project on Artificial Intelligence, was an eight-week gathering at Dartmouth College in Hanover, New Hampshire, that ran from mid-June to mid-August 1956.[^s4] It is widely regarded as the event that launched artificial intelligence as a research discipline, and the proposal written to fund it is where the phrase "artificial intelligence" first appeared.[^s2][^s4][^s5] John McCarthy, who drafted that proposal while an assistant professor at Dartmouth, is credited with coining the term in 1955.[^s3]

## The proposal

The proposal, dated 31 August 1955, was signed by four organizers from four institutions: McCarthy (Dartmouth mathematics), Marvin Minsky (a Harvard Junior Fellow in mathematics and neurology), Nathaniel Rochester (manager of information research at IBM) and Claude Shannon (mathematician at Bell Telephone Laboratories).[^s1] It asked the Rockefeller Foundation to support a two-month study by about ten researchers the following summer.[^s1] Its central premise was a bold conjecture: that every aspect of learning, or any other feature of intelligence, could in principle be described precisely enough for a machine to simulate it.[^s1][^s5] The organizers hoped a carefully chosen group working together for one summer could make significant progress on getting machines to use language, form abstractions and concepts, solve problems then reserved for humans, and improve themselves.[^s1]

The document grouped the "artificial intelligence problem" into seven areas: automatic computers, programming a computer to use a language, neuron nets, a theory of the size of a calculation, self-improvement, abstractions, and randomness and creativity.[^s1] It requested a budget of $13,500, mostly salaries for faculty-level participants and graduate students plus travel; IBM and Bell Labs were expected to pay for their own staff.[^s1] Each proposer also sketched a personal research plan; McCarthy's, for example, was to design an artificial language in which a computer could state and test conjectures and refer to itself.[^s1]

## As it happened

McCarthy later wrote that the event did not match the plan. The Foundation funded only about half the request, and rather than collaborating on one agenda for two months, participants pursued their own research, arriving and leaving at different times.[^s2] Only McCarthy, Minsky and Ray Solomonoff stayed for the full eight weeks; attendance totalled a few dozen people over the summer, including Oliver Selfridge.[^s4]

The highlight, by McCarthy's account, was a brief visit by Allen Newell and Herbert Simon, who presented their Logic Theorist program.[^s2][^s4] Alex Bernstein described a chess program, and Minsky's idea for a geometry theorem prover led Herbert Gelernter to build a list-processing language for it, part of the line of work that preceded McCarthy's 1958 proposal of Lisp.[^s2][^s3] Solomonoff's probabilistic approach to induction, discussed there, later became one of the foundations of machine learning.[^s4]

## Significance and critiques

The workshop's main result was institutional rather than technical: it named a field, gave it an agenda and connected the people who would found its first laboratories.[^s2] It also exposed an early split between approaches modelled on the brain, such as neuron nets, and those built on symbolic logic and heuristic search, a tension that runs through AI's later history.[^s1][^s4] Its emphasis on heuristics and algorithmic problem solving shaped the work for which Newell and Simon later received the 1975 Turing Award.[^s4]

The meeting is also a standard example of over-optimism in AI. The proposal expected major progress in a single summer, and McCarthy himself said decades later that the hoped-for breakthrough toward human-level intelligence did not happen there and that new ideas were still needed.[^s1][^s2] Historians also note that the "birth of AI" framing is partly retrospective: much of the most cited work presented, like the Logic Theorist, was developed elsewhere and only shown briefly at Dartmouth.[^s2][^s4] Dartmouth still presents the meeting as the seminal event of the field and has built later AI initiatives on that legacy.[^s5]

## Connections

- **Broader:** the workshop is the conventional starting point of **artificial intelligence** as a named discipline.[^s2]
- **Created by:** it was organized by **John McCarthy**, who coined the term, and **Marvin Minsky**, alongside Rochester and Shannon.[^s1][^s3]
- **Studied there:** the **Logic Theorist** of Newell and Simon was the best-received work presented.[^s2]
- **Related:** the proposal's "neuron nets" topic links it to **artificial neural network** research, while the heuristic, logic-based programs it showcased became the core of **symbolic AI**.[^s1][^s4] It followed Turing's 1950 framing of machine intelligence in the **Turing test** by only a few years, but set out an engineering programme rather than a behavioural criterion.[^s1]
